## Rules

### Closed questions
Use a yes/no or pick-one question only when you need one fact. To get stories, ask an open question, or follow a closed one with "what makes you say that?".
- Weak: "Do you like the form?" Better: "Tell me about the last time you filled in the form."
- Source M5: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed-ended_question
- Quote: "It is often argued that open-ended questions (i.e. questions that elicit more than a yes/no answers) are preferable because they open up discussion and enquiry."

### Leading questions
Do not hint at the answer you hope for. Ask about what the person did or thought, and leave out your own opinion and loaded words.
- Weak: "The new menu is easier to use, isn't it?" Better: "How did you find your way around the menu?"
- Source M3: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leading_question
- Quote: "A leading question is a question that suggests a particular answer and contains information the examiner is looking to have confirmed."
- Also M1: https://www.ontario.ca/page/user-interviews
- Quote: "Avoid leading questions. A good interviewer will focus on what the participant wants to express rather than leading, imposing or implying certain views onto the interviewee."
- Also M2: https://edis.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/WC338
- Quote: "Try not to place any judgments on what the interviewee is saying."

### Double-barreled questions
One question, one idea. If a question has "and" joining two topics, split it in two.
- Weak: "How easy and how useful was the service?" Better: "How easy was the service to use?" then, separately, "How useful was it to you?"
- Source M4: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double-barreled_question
- Quote: "It is committed when someone asks a question that touches upon more than one issue, yet allows only for one answer."

### Plain language
Use words your participant uses. Skip jargon and long sentences; test the question on someone like your participant.
- Weak: "How do you evaluate the efficacy of the onboarding workflow?" Better: "What was it like the first time you started using it?"
- Source M6: https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/principles/
- Quote: "Use language your audience understands and feels comfortable with."

### Probes
Plan a short follow-up for when an answer is thin: ask for an example or "tell me more", and keep it neutral. Avoid probes that put words in the person's mouth.
- Weak: "So that was frustrating?" Better: "Can you give me an example of what happened?"
- Source M1: https://www.ontario.ca/page/user-interviews
- Quote: "Can you tell me what you thought it meant?"
- Also M2 (probe wording such as "Tell me more about that" is from my reading of the page; not quoted).

### Consent
Say why you are asking and what you will do with the answers. Get agreement before you record or take notes.
- Weak: starting the recorder without a word. Better: "I'd like to record so I can listen again later; is that all right?"
- Source M2: https://edis.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/WC338
- Quote: "If you do not collect a formal informed consent, ask permission to record the interview and take notes."
- Also, to reassure the person, M2:
- Quote: "Be sure to emphasize that there are no wrong answers to your questions"

### Timing
Plan for about an hour at most, leave room for the person to talk, and keep a closing question for the end.
- Weak: 40 questions in 30 minutes. Better: 8 main questions with spare time for follow-ups.
- Source M1: https://www.ontario.ca/page/user-interviews
- Quote: "You typically want to keep your interviews within an hour."
- Also M2: https://edis.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/WC338
- Quote: "Finish, time permitting, with a question such as"

### Ordering
Start broad and easy, put facts before opinions, and ask the sensitive things later once there is trust.
- Weak: opening with "How angry were you about the fee?" Better: "Walk me through what happened with the fee", and feelings after.
- Source M2: https://edis.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/WC338
- Quote: "If you are going to ask about both facts and opinions, start with the factual questions."
- Also M1 (page advises starting with general questions; the exact line "Start with general questions." was read but is not machine-checked here).

### Past behaviour
Ask what the person actually did, last time, not what they think they would do. Avoid "Would you...?" and "How much would you pay?" questions; people guess badly about the future and about their own past. If memory is needed, start with the most recent time and work backward.
- Weak: "Would you pay £4 a month for a car scent?" Better: "Tell me about the last time you bought something for your car's smell. What did you pick, and what did it cost?"
- Source M7: https://mtlynch.io/book-reports/the-mom-test/
- Quote: "Ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future."
- Also M8: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/interviewing-users/
- Quote: "Having users misremember the past or mispredict the future should both be shunned."
- Also M10: https://measuringu.com/six-ways-to-improve-recall/
- Quote: "consider asking about the most recent occurrences first and working backward."
- Note (my reading of M8, not quoted): the page warns that recall is imperfect, so keep the window short and ask for concrete events.
- Note (my reading of M7, not quoted): people overstate what they would pay; ask what they spend now.

### Sample size
Six to eight interviews can show the main themes if the people are similar, but plan a few more if you hear different stories. Keep the sample honest: note who was asked and who agreed, and include people who are not enthusiastic.
- Weak: "Five friends said yes, so customers want it." Better: "Invite from the whole customer list, write down who was asked and who came, and stop when new interviews bring nothing new."
- Source M9: https://citedrive.com/en/discovery/how-many-interviews-are-enough/ (abstract page only)
- Quote: "basic elements for metathemes were present as early as six interviews"
- Also M12: https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Social_Work_and_Human_Services/Social_Science_Research_-_Principles_Methods_and_Practices_%28Bhattacherjee%29/09:_Survey_Research/9.04:_Biases_in_Survey_Research
- Quote: "dissatisfied customers tend to be more vocal about their experience than satisfied customers"
- Note (my reading of M9, not quoted): the same abstract says saturation came within the first twelve of sixty interviews; the study is one set of similar interviewees, so six is a floor to watch, not a promise.
- Note (my reading of M12): the page is about surveys; the point about who chooses to answer carries over to who agrees to be interviewed.

### Asking about smell (words)
People find scents hard to put into words, so do not expect or demand exact descriptions. Offer examples to react to, or ask what it reminded them of.
- Weak: "Describe the scent in three words." Better: "What does it remind you of, if anything?"
- Source M11: https://www.kpbs.org/news/2018/jan/18/that-smells-like-um-i-cant-think-of-a-word-for-it
- Quote: "it's very difficult to describe scents in English."
- Note: this is a news article, not an interview method; it supports the caution only, and no source here gives a method for asking about smell.

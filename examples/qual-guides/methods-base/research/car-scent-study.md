## Q1. Why is "would you buy it?" a poor question, and what to ask instead?

### R1. Michael Lynch, "The Mom Test" (book report)
- Link: https://mtlynch.io/book-reports/the-mom-test/
- Author: Michael Lynch (review of Rob Fitzpatrick's book *The Mom Test*)
- Licence, as the page states: Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 (CC BY 4.0), © 2026
- Summary: People say yes to a made-up purchase because it costs them nothing. Ask about specific things they did in the past, such as how they handle the problem today, rather than about opinions or the future. This is a second-hand summary of the book, not the book.
- Quote: "People are often overly optimistic about paying for a product when it's hypothetical."
- Quote: "Ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future."

### R2. Jakob Nielsen, "Interviewing Users" (Nielsen Norman Group, 2010)
- Link: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/interviewing-users/
- Author: Jakob Nielsen
- Licence, as the page states: "Copyright © 1998-2026 Nielsen Norman Group, All Rights Reserved." Link and short quotes only.
- Summary: People cannot predict how they would use something they have not seen, and they also forget past detail; the safest ground is what they are doing now or did recently.
- Quote: "Similarly, you can't ask "Would you use (potential future) feature X?" because users can't predict something they haven't seen."
- Quote: "Having users misremember the past or mispredict the future should both be shunned."

For the draft question "Would you pay £4 a month": both sources say replace it with what they bought, used or did about car smell last time.

## Q2. How many conversations are enough? Is six to eight believable?

### R3. Guest, Bunce and Johnson (2006), "How Many Interviews Are Enough?" (abstract page)
- Link: https://citedrive.com/en/discovery/how-many-interviews-are-enough/
- Authors: Greg Guest, Arwen Bunce, Laura Johnson (DOI 10.1177/1525822x05279903; the page does not name the journal)
- Licence, as the page states: none stated. Only the abstract was read, not the full paper.
- Summary: In one study of 60 interviews with a fairly similar group, most themes had appeared by twelve interviews, and the main outline by six. So six to eight is believable for one fairly similar group and a broad question, as a first look; it does not show you have found everything. Limits: one study, one setting; and your group (people who bought a car scent) may differ in car type, habits and why they bought.
- Quote: "saturation occurred within the first twelve interviews"
- Quote: "basic elements for metathemes were present as early as six interviews"

Not found: a source on sizing a study around one cheap £2,500 decision. Search results also named a 2016 follow-up (journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1525822X16640447) saying about 16 or fewer for similar groups; I could not open it, so I use nothing from it.

## Q3. Asking about a habit without leading, and how far back people remember

### R4. Jeff Sauro and Jim Lewis, "Six Ways to Improve Participant Recall" (MeasuringU, 10 Aug 2021)
- Link: https://measuringu.com/six-ways-to-improve-recall/
- Authors: Jeff Sauro, Jim Lewis
- Licence, as the page states: "© 2026 MeasuringU®" only; no reuse licence stated. Link and short quotes only.
- Summary: Ask about a short, recent window, begin with the most recent time and go backwards, and add concrete cues to the question. They give no hard number of days; "yesterday, last week, last month" are the easy ranges. Routine, low-interest acts (like a car smell) fade faster than notable ones, which is my reading and not stated on the page.
- Quote: "In general, it's easier to recall what you did yesterday, last week, or the last month"
- Quote: "consider asking about the most recent occurrences first and working backward."

R2 (https://www.nngroup.com/articles/interviewing-users/) adds the cost of waiting: people forget specific detail.
- Quote: "Human memory is fallible"

Not found: a freely readable page that gives a firm recall limit for everyday buying. Ask "the last time" and keep to the last few weeks as a working rule; that limit is my inference.

## Q4. Talking about smell, and helping people find words without giving them

### R5. Angus Chen, "That Smells Like ... Um, I Can't Think Of A Word For It!" (NPR, via KPBS)
- Link: https://www.kpbs.org/news/2018/jan/18/that-smells-like-um-i-cant-think-of-a-word-for-it
- Author: Angus Chen (NPR); reports work by Asifa Majid
- Licence, as the page states: "Copyright 2018 NPR." Link and short quotes only.
- Summary: English speakers find smell hard to put into words. Expect a few answers like "it smells like X" or a liking/disliking, not precise words.
- Quote: "it's very difficult to describe scents in English."

### R6. NPR with Jay Gottfried, "Words Fail When It Comes To Aromas" (via KSJD, 2014)
- Link: https://www.ksjd.org/2014-11-16/words-fail-when-it-comes-to-aromas
- Author: Rachel Martin interviewing Jay Gottfried (NPR)
- Licence, as the page states: copyright NPR. Link and short quotes only.
- Summary: Even common smells are hard to name when other cues are removed.
- Quote: "you'll have great difficulty naming them all."

### R7. Kyle Gunn (2026), "What we talk about when we talk about smells: a corpus study of the language of olfaction" (PhD thesis, University of Glasgow)
- Link: https://theses.gla.ac.uk/85812/
- Author: Kyle Gunn
- Licence, as the page states: "Copyright of this thesis is held by the author." No open licence. Abstract only.
- Summary: When people do describe smell, they mostly name the source or give a feeling or association, not a direct smell word. So "what does it remind you of?" and "where have you smelled it?" fit how people talk.
- Quote: "a primary strategy for communicating olfactive meaning is through connotation and association"
- Quote: "source-based descriptors are employed in olfactory description"

What helps without giving the words: no source I opened tests an interview method. My suggestion, not a sourced rule: ask what it reminds them of, where they smelled it, and when it was strong or faint; avoid offering lists such as "fresh, clean, sweet?". Only an older paid article (SCC, not readable) said word lists can sway answers.

## Q5. Recruiting past customers without hearing only the happy ones

### R8. Anol Bhattacherjee, "9.4: Biases in Survey Research" (Social Science Research: Principles, Methods, and Practices)
- Link: https://socialsci.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Social_Work_and_Human_Services/Social_Science_Research_-_Principles_Methods_and_Practices_%28Bhattacherjee%29/09:_Survey_Research/9.04:_Biases_in_Survey_Research
- Author: Anol Bhattacherjee, University of South Florida
- Licence, as the page states: CC BY-SA 3.0
- Summary: People who reply to a request are not like the people who do not; the page notes unhappy customers are more likely to speak up. That cuts against a worry about only hearing happy ones, but the point stands: the volunteers are not typical. Invite from the whole customer list, record who was asked and who agreed, and include some who stopped buying or never replied (my practical reading). This is about surveys, not interviews.
- Quote: "dissatisfied customers tend to be more vocal about their experience than satisfied customers"

Not found: a freely readable source on recruiting for small interview studies from a shop's own customers.

### Gap 3. How many interviews for a £2,500 decision; the 2016 follow-up
No source found that sizes a study by the money at stake. The 2016 paper by Guest, Namey and McKenna (SAGE, 10.1177/1525822X16640447) still returns 403, so nothing is used from it. A different freely readable 2016 paper, which tests Guest and others, is below.

#### S2. Hennink, Kaiser and Marconi, "Code Saturation Versus Meaning Saturation: How Many Interviews Are Enough?" (Qualitative Health Research)
- Link: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9359070
- Authors: Monique M. Hennink, Bonnie N. Kaiser, Vincent C. Marconi
- Licence, as the page states: none stated (PMC author manuscript; links to PMC copyright notice).
- Summary: In 25 interviews with one fairly similar group, the list of issues was mostly found by nine interviews, but fully understanding them took 16 to 24. The authors say a more varied group needs more, and that Guest's twelve should not be used as a general number. So six to eight interviews will show the main points of a similar group but may leave meanings unclear; for a £2,500 decision, treat them as a first look and plan to add a few if answers still surprise you (my reading, not on the page).
- Quote: "code saturation was reached at nine interviews"
- Quote: "16 to 24 interviews were needed to reach meaning saturation"

### Gap 5. Who to talk to first, and reaching people who never bought
#### S3. Aha! Labs, "How to choose the right customers for product discovery interviews"
- Link: https://www.aha.io/roadmapping/guide/how-to-choose-the-right-customers-for-product-discovery-interviews
- Author: no person named; Aha! Labs Inc. (updated August 2025, as the page states)
- Licence, as the page states: "© 2026 Aha! Labs Inc. • All rights reserved". Link and short quotes only.
- Summary: Interview people who match your target but have not adopted the product, as well as customers; with a small or new customer base, look in communities where they gather, at natural contact points, and through your network. Recent leavers can explain why people stop. It is a company guide for software, not research.
- Quote: "do not have a large customer base yet"
- Quote: "potential users who match your target personas, but have not adopted the product yet"

#### S4. eChai Ventures, "Where do I find people to interview when I have zero audience"
- Link: https://echai.ventures/startingup/customer-research/where-do-i-find-people-to-interview-when-i-have-zero-audience
- Author: no person named on the page; eChai Ventures
- Licence, as the page states: "© 2026 eChai Ventures"; no open licence. Link and short quotes only.
- Summary: Begin with people you know who have the problem and ask each for introductions; go where such people already gather; ask to learn, not to sell; paid panels are the fallback.
- Quote: "Warm intros beat cold blasts every time."

Answer to the owner's question, from S3 and S4 plus the first note: both groups can help. Start with the once-only buyers, since you can reach them and they can describe a real purchase (past behaviour, R1). Add a few people who bought something else or nothing, found through staff, friends of customers or car groups, to hear why they did not buy scent. How many of each is my suggestion; no source gives a split. A Forrester post on non-customers (forrester.com/blogs/identifying-noncustomers-for-customer-interviews) returned 404 and is not used.
